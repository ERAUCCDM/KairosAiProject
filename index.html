An Introduction by Dr. Emily Dux Speltz

It is my pleasure to introduce this project, which brings together reflections on generative artificial intelligence from a learning community of faculty across Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University's Humanities and Communication departments on our three main U.S. campuses (Daytona Beach, Florida; Prescott, Arizona; and our Worldwide online campus). As a scholar of applied linguistics and technology who specializes in the writing process and writing technologies, I have spent the past few years fully immersed in conversations about AI. It has guided my research and course designs; I have presented pedagogical recommendations for colleagues enough times that I feel bad for anyone who has to listen to me share my thoughts about AI for a single minute more; I have given conference presentations to crowds that love AI and crowds that hate AI; and I have lurked but avoided commenting on far too many controversial LinkedIn threads to count. It is an exciting, daunting, and sometimes overwhelming time to be an educator, especially one who teaches writing. So when my department chair, Dr. David Higgins, suggested that I bring together colleagues in our field from across our university to tackle these issues collectively, I was happy to make that happen.

What emerged from that idea is the project you see here: a collaborative effort that spans our three campuses and brings together a group of faculty who, over the course of the past year, have met regularly to read, discuss, and reflect on the complex intersections of AI, communication, and pedagogy. I have curated a set of scholarly readings designed to push our thinking in different directions: sometimes challenging our assumptions about what "writing" or "teaching" means in the new era of generative AI, and sometimes revealing the unexpected opportunities that new technologies might hold for teaching and learning. Through these conversations, we have wrestled with difficult questions about authorship, creativity, assessment, and the role of human judgment.

As you explore this collection, you will meet part of the vibrant group of faculty who have joined me in this work. You will find that these are colleagues who bring wit, curiosity, and a healthy dose of skepticism to every conversation. The videos here capture both the humor and the seriousness of our endeavor: we are trying to make sense of something enormous and fast-moving, and we have had a lot of fun doing it, despite the chaos. We hope that this collection offers both a glimpse into our process and an invitation for you to form your own community of inquiry, to read and talk together, and to chart your own path through this strange and fascinating moment in writing and communication studies. Alongside our reflections, you will also find a practical guide with the readings and discussion topics we used, in case you would like to carry out a similar project at your own institution.

Literature Review

Challenges Facing Writing Instructors Amid the Rise of Generative AI

Pedagogical Uncertainty and the Pace of Technological Change

A dominant theme in the emerging literature on generative AI in education is the profound pedagogical uncertainty experienced by instructors, particularly those teaching writing-intensive courses. Student adoption has been rapid: in a 2025 survey of UK undergraduates, 92% reported using AI tools and 88% reported using generative AI for assessments (Freeman, 2025). Instructors, by contrast, report considerable uncertainty. In a U.S. national survey, only 14% of postsecondary instructors felt confident using generative AI in their teaching, and more than half were undecided about whether it would benefit teaching in their fields (Ruediger et al., 2024). Course policies, meanwhile, vary widely: 42% of instructors in the same survey prohibited student use of generative AI entirely, with prohibition most common in the humanities (Ruediger et al., 2024), and writing studies’ professional organizations warn that variation in policies among instructors, departments, and institutions can leave students unsure what will be acceptable in their next class (MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI, 2023). The “right way” is unclear.

From a writing studies perspective, this uncertainty is especially consequential because composition pedagogy has long emphasized intentional design of composing processes, not merely the evaluation of final products. As a result, instructors face difficulty determining where learning is occurring – if it is occurring – how to intervene productively, and how to align AI-mediated composing practices with established pedagogical goals.

Cognitive and Rhetorical Impacts on Writing Development

Beyond immediate instructional challenges, scholars increasingly raise concerns about the cognitive and rhetorical consequences of sustained AI use for developing writers. While AI tools can produce fluent, well-structured prose, that fluency may mask reduced cognitive engagement. In an exploratory EEG study of essay writing, posted as a preprint, participants who used ChatGPT showed the weakest neural connectivity of three groups, reported the lowest sense of ownership of their essays, and had difficulty accurately quoting what they had just written (Kosmyna et al., 2025). Writing studies has long held that writing is a mode of learning rather than a mere transcription of ideas (MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI, 2023); when idea generation, organization, and stylistic decisions are partially or wholly outsourced to AI, students may bypass the productive struggle through which that learning occurs. Survey evidence points the same way: heavier AI tool use is associated with greater cognitive offloading and lower critical-thinking scores, though the relationship is correlational (Gerlich, 2025).

These concerns align closely with threshold concepts in writing studies, particularly “writing is a social and rhetorical activity” and “revision is central to developing writing” (Adler-Kassner & Wardle, 2015). Without explicit instructional framing, AI tools risk reinforcing the view that writing is primarily about efficiency rather than meaning-making; Baron (2023) warns that this trade-off threatens writing’s role in clarifying thought and in expressing a writer’s own voice. The result could be writing that is polished but flat. In one experiment, short stories written with AI-generated ideas were rated more creative and better written, yet were more similar to one another than stories written without AI (Doshi & Hauser, 2024).

Instructor Preparedness and Professional Development Gaps

Another persistent theme in the literature is the lack of instructor preparedness, especially among writing instructors tasked with frontline responses to student AI use. Writing studies’ professional organizations warn that faculty may lack adequate support and up-to-date training on how generative AI systems function (MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI, 2023). This knowledge gap constrains instructors’ ability to engage students in critical discussions about AI outputs, authorship, and credibility.

The problem is exacerbated by labor conditions within writing programs. Contingent instructors—adjuncts, graduate teaching assistants, and full-time non-tenure-track faculty—make up the vast majority of college writing instructors and often work in precarious, under-supported conditions, yet they may be asked to make significant changes to their teaching without adequate time, training, or compensation (MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI, 2023). The task force further warns that contingent faculty may be excluded from institutional decision-making about generative AI while still being expected to carry out labor-intensive AI policies and to teach critical AI literacy.

Implications for Transfer and Writing Beyond the Classroom

Writing transfer research shows that students often encounter roadblocks when moving writing knowledge across contexts, and that metacognitive awareness frequently plays a key role in successful transfer; researchers therefore recommend that instructors explicitly foster it (Center for Engaged Learning, 2015). Generative AI complicates this challenge by performing rhetorical and stylistic labor on students’ behalf, potentially reducing opportunities for learners to internalize adaptable writing strategies.

If students come to view AI as a universal solution to writing tasks, they may fail to recognize the contextual nature of writing across disciplines and professional settings. The MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI (2023) warns that students may miss writing and thinking practice when they submit AI output as their own work, and that students may stop seeing writing as valuable when machines can mimic it; the task force also cautions that the public may come to believe AI replaces the process knowledge and metacognition that writing helps develop. Such losses could undermine students’ ability to adapt writing knowledge in novel situations—precisely the outcome that first-year writing and writing-in-the-disciplines programs aim to cultivate.

Future-Oriented Risks and the Underestimation of Impact

A recurring theme in the literature is that the long-term consequences of AI for writing instruction remain poorly understood; a recent review of 2023–2025 research describes much of that literature as still in its infancy (Sanz-Tejeda et al., 2026). Educators’ earliest concerns centered on plagiarism and the inability to detect AI use, yet writing scholars caution that detection tools are unreliable, can produce false accusations, and push teachers toward punishing students rather than supporting them (MLA-CCCC Joint Task Force on Writing and AI, 2023). Classroom experiments that require students to write with AI surface questions detection cannot settle: what counts as plagiarism, and how AI changes our understanding of writing, authenticity, and creativity (Fyfe, 2023). Writing has historically served as a mechanism for cultivating intellectual responsibility and disciplinary identity; if AI becomes an unexamined co-author, these functions may be fundamentally altered.

What We Still Don’t Know: Methodological Gaps and Future Directions

Despite a rapidly growing body of conceptual scholarship, significant methodological gaps remain. Existing studies are dominated by cross-sectional surveys of perceptions and attitudes, and reviewers have called for research measuring how AI affects critical thinking and academic writing over the medium and long term (Sanz-Tejeda et al., 2026). Few studies yet track how sustained AI use influences rhetorical decision-making, metacognitive awareness, or authorship across time. Process-oriented research is also limited, as AI externalizes composing work traditionally visible through drafts and revision histories.

Beyond early program-level accounts, most research examines the student perspective, with only a few studies including instructors (Sanz-Tejeda et al., 2026); fewer still are instructor-focused empirical studies documenting how writing faculty understand AI systems, redesign assignments, and engage in collective sense-making. Finally, there is limited research explicitly grounded in threshold concepts and transfer theory examining how AI mediates students’ internalization of writing knowledge. Addressing these gaps will require longitudinal, mixed-methods, and community-based research designs.

Bios

Emily Dux Speltz, Ph.D., is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Humanities & Communication at Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University Worldwide. She received her Ph.D. in Applied Linguistics & Technology from Iowa State University. Dr. Dux Speltz's research explores applications of innovative technologies—including generative artificial intelligence, keystroke logging, and eye tracking—in the writing process and education.

David M. Higgins is associate professor of English and American Studies and chair of the Department of Humanities and Communication at Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University Worldwide. He is also the president of the International Association for the Fantastic in the Arts, and he serves as a senior editor for the Los Angeles Review of Books. David is the author of Reverse Colonization: Science Fiction, Imperial Fantasy, and Alt-Victimhood, which won the 2021 Science Fiction Research Association Book Award. He has also published a critical monograph examining Ann Leckie's SF masterwork Ancillary Justice, and his research has been published in journals such as American Literature, Science Fiction Studies, Paradoxa, and Extrapolation.

David serves as a Fellow with the University of Minnesota's Center for Climate Literacy and an editor for Climate Lit, an open-access compendium of environmental literacy resources for educators. In the public sphere, David has been a featured speaker on NPR's radio show On Point, and his literary journalism has been published in the Los Angeles Review of Books and The Guardian.

Dr. Lynn Koller is an associate professor of Humanities and Communication at Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University. She studies the relationship between technology, culture, and communication, with a particular interest in how emerging technologies reshape the ways people make meaning. Her work has also moved outside the university through Project Ataraxia, a jail-based humanities program she developed to explore how writing and creative work might matter in places where education is scarce. Her recent work explores artificial intelligence in higher education, not to settle the debate but to create more chaos. She is a cat person.

Dr. Alex Rister is an assistant professor of Communication and Program Chair for the Bachelor of Science in Communication Embry-Riddle Aeronautical University’s Worldwide Campus. She holds M.A. degrees in both Communication and English and a Ph.D. in Texts and Technology from the University of Central Florida. Her research aligns with her professional service and community volunteer work on communication for social change, especially on issues important to girls and women. As a prior University of Central Florida Francis Bok Human Trafficking Awareness Fellow, much of her work focuses on human trafficking awareness.
